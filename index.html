Leigh Thompson — Negotiation Strategies

I run on data, so I had assumed the advantage gets built at the table: know more, read faster, win. Thompson’s point is that by the time you are in the room, the balance of power is already set — and what happens next is mostly the execution of preparation you either did or skipped. Which is an uncomfortable thing to hear if you have been quietly proud of improvising well.

The specific thing I had skipped

The other side’s alternatives.

I am good at knowing my own numbers. I had almost never done the work of figuring out what happens to the person across the table if we do not do the deal — which is the number that actually sets the price. Fifteen years of thinking I had an information advantage, built on information that was only ever half the board.

Classroom III — Marketing Analytics: Leading with Big Data · Eric Anderson

Not deeper. Not shallower. A different axis.

I have spent fifteen years on this. I expected the course to either impress me or bore me. It did neither — it was pointed ninety degrees away from where I point.

Where Kellogg points

Who has to believe it

Which decision changes, who owns that decision, and what evidence would actually move them. The model is an input to a persuasion problem. We worked on real retail and consumer-goods sales data — which, after years inside a retailer, I thought I knew how to read.

Where I point

How the machine gets built — and stays alive

Pipelines, governance, headcount, the thing still running in eighteen months. The persuasion is an input to a system problem.

Both are right, and neither is complete on its own. If you work in tech, data or AI, this is the week the EMBA stops being general management and becomes your problem — argued at you by 130 people who all need what you build.

People, part two

Naval science. Then finance.
Then law school.

One classmate is an associate professor at Northwestern, where he teaches naval science. He got interested in finance, so he enrolled in the EMBA. After this he wants to go to law school.

Military science, then corporate finance, then law. Said out loud in that order it sounds like indecision. Spend twenty minutes with him and it sounds like the most deliberate career in the room — and it made my own “what do I become next” question feel a great deal less dramatic.

The most interesting thing in that room was never the job titles. It was how people got to them.

The two lines you will hear all week
Low ego.High impact.

Every school has a slogan. Here is the one thing that convinced me this one is real: in a room of 130 senior people, “I don’t know” was said constantly — cheerfully, with no hedging and no visible embarrassment. Founders said it. PhDs said it. Nobody lost a millimetre of standing for it.

If you have built a career somewhere that admitting it costs you something, that alone is worth the flight.

What “networking” actually looked like

Nobody exchanged a business card. Everyone exchanged a problem.

[ Late Lounge / the boat / breakfast table — one photo ]
Uncertainty never stays in the case study.

The best conversation of my week was not at the bar. It was in the crisis classroom, on the word uncertainty. We started on how a company absorbs it — and inside twenty minutes we were talking about our own: careers, money, families, the decisions none of us can model. Same framework, different scale. That is what a room of senior people from four continents does to a technical topic. It refuses to leave it technical.

The building does half the teaching.

The Global Hub is the thing I keep describing to people who ask. All glass and curves, wrapped around an atrium, with the lagoon on one side and Lake Michigan behind it. You walk in and the building tells you what kind of week this is going to be — open, exposed, nowhere to sit at the back.

The return flight

Three things went into the carry-on

Two of them are useful. The third one is the reason I would go again.

In that room, being the person who can actually build it was not the scarce thing.
The one that mattered

For fifteen years my identity has been technical: the one who knows what the data can and cannot do. Eight days with 130 senior people from four continents made it clear that this identity has a ceiling, and that I had been standing under it comfortably.

So the question I flew home with is not what should I become. It is narrower and harder than that: how do you actually build the bridge between the technology and the business? I do not have the answer. I now have a much better version of the question.

Also — a method I now use

Write the trade-off into the proposal

Chopra spent two days on one idea: refusing to name the trade-off does not make it disappear. Every proposal I have written since Evanston states what we are giving up, in writing, on the page. It makes the conversation shorter and the decision better.

If you are thinking about applying

Six honest things, no brochure language

01
Your English is probably fine. That was never the bar.
Understanding the room is not the same as being in it. The bar is whether you will speak — whether you push into a discussion running in five accents at once instead of nodding along. A classroom that genuinely fuses cultures from all over the world is rare, and it only pays you back if you get in.
02
The load is attention, not language.
Full days, back to back. You are listening hard to the professor and just as hard to 130 classmates arguing, all day, for eight days. That sustained concentration is the real cost — far more than the English.
03
Raise your hand on day one. Get it over with.
Speak in the first session, before the room has settled into who talks and who does not. Once you have said something out loud, the rest of the eight days is easy. Wait until Wednesday and you will still be waiting on Friday.
04
My mistake: I optimised for the assignments
Jet lag plus doing the homework properly meant I skipped the evening drinks. Operations had just spent two days teaching us that every choice is a trade-off — and I made this one without ever admitting to myself that I was making it. The coursework was gradeable. The other thing was not. Do the reading on the plane.
05
Who should not come
Anyone who cannot go dark for eight consecutive days. If you have to stay reachable, you will be physically present and functionally absent — and with the groups reshuffling every course, everyone notices.
06
Was it worth it? I signed up for four global electives.
Four is the maximum you are allowed to take. That is my honest answer — I did not go home and write a reflection, I went home and booked the next four countries.
Last thing

Come with curiosity.
Leave with a global family.

One year in, eight days in Evanston, and that map of six campuses stopped being a map. It became a list of people I can call — and four elective countries already on my calendar.
